Jan 2025 — Present · Dynamics and Control Lab, University of South Carolina

Robust Control Framework for Retractable Tether Multirotor UAVs

Developing a controller capable of maintaining UAV stability through the slack-to-taut tether transition — a controls problem that currently has no reliable solution — enabling expanded use of RTMUAVs in high-risk persistent aerial operations.

Problem

Tethered UAV systems offer compelling advantages for persistent operations, but retractable tethers introduce a fundamentally difficult stability problem: the system must remain controlled as the tether dynamically transitions between a slack (unconstrained) and taut (tension-loaded) state. No controller framework capable of handling both states robustly in high-risk situations existed prior to this work.

System Modeling

  • Developed a fully coupled dynamic model of the UAV, tether, and payload as a unified system, capturing nonlinear interactions across both tether states
  • Tether modeled under both slack and taut conditions using a spring-damper model and multilink representation for improved fidelity
  • State-switching logic with adaptive gains to handle transition dynamics that destabilize conventional controllers

Controller Design

  • Geometric controller with adaptive gains validated in MATLAB against wind disturbances and sensor noise
  • Implemented Low Frequency Learning (LFL) filter — filters high-frequency noise by comparison to a weight estimate, eliminating leakage bias
  • LFL outperforms standard MRAC: tracks more precisely, requires less control rate effort, at the same learning rate
  • Constructed Python simulation environment mapping expected flight envelopes to safety/clearance regions

Disturbance Modeling

  • 8-tone gust field applied throughout testing
  • Sensor noise included in tension feedback term
  • Randomized simulations used to extract 95th-percentile excursion envelopes across tether lengths of 2–6 m

Reeling Platform

  • Novel reeling system under 500–800 g tracking tether tension and direction in real time
  • Two-axis gimbal with rotary sensors for tether direction; reel-rotation sensor for deployment and retrieval tracking
  • Provides near-end tension values needed for onboard controller calculations

Jun 2026 — Oct 2026 · Personal Project

CFD Study of Crosswind / Yaw Effect on F1 Aerodynamics

Five OpenFOAM simulations across 0–8° yaw angles on a CAD model built to 2026 F1 regulations, studying how crosswinds affect downforce, drag, and aerodynamic center.

Motivation

Formula 1 represents the extreme edge of aerodynamic engineering — where tenths-of-a-second performance margins matter. This personal project was designed to build deeper understanding of F1 aerodynamics beyond typical straight-line analysis, specifically studying how crosswinds affect overall car behavior.

CAD Model Development

  • Built using CATIA Generative Shape Design to 2026 F1 regulations, with some lenient constraints
  • Focused on downforce and drag considerations for the underbody, front wing, and rear wing
  • Design cross-referenced against comparable team models to validate geometry

CFD Setup

  • All simulations run in OpenFOAM
  • Car velocity: 80,000 mm/s; airflow velocity held at constant magnitude across yaw angles to conserve dynamic pressure
  • Rotating MRFs applied to all four wheels
  • kOmegaSST turbulence model selected to capture vortex development, flow separation, and viscous effects under yawing conditions
  • Ground velocity matched to car y-velocity only

Results

  • CL and CD both increase proportionally with yaw angle, with CD increasing slightly faster
  • Causes a slight downward drift in L/D ratio across the range
  • Conclusion: crosswinds up to 8° have minimal effect on overall L/D; primary effects are on yawing moments, lateral forces, and aerodynamic center shift

2021 — 2023 · Spring Valley High School

Early Research

Two independent research projects conducted in high school, both presented at competitive science fairs and recognized with awards.

Diesel Particulate Filter Soot Removal — 2022/23

Designed and built a small-scale test apparatus to evaluate whether a charged particulate filter could more efficiently remove soot from diesel particulate filters. Used carbon black as a soot surrogate, steel wool electrodes, and a 3D-printed honeycomb DPF model.

  • Developed hands-on skills in soldering, circuit design, and 3D printing through end-to-end hardware fabrication
  • Identified more realistic flow conditions as the key variable requiring future investigation
  • Presented at SCJAS 2023, JSHS 2024, and UofSC Science & Engineering Fair 2023

Nanoparticle Toxicity on Soil & Plant Growth — 2021/22

Designed a controlled 3-week exposure experiment assessing MgO/TiO₂ nanoparticle mixture toxicity across two concentration levels, measuring soil pH, salinity, and plant height in Vigna radiata.

  • Found consistent adverse effects — increased soil pH and reduced plant growth — at both concentration levels
  • Contributed environmental impact data relevant to agricultural nanoparticle applications
  • Presented at SCJAS 2022 and UofSC Science & Engineering Fair 2022
  • Received Metric System Award at SCJAS 2022
