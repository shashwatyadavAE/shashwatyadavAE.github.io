Jan 2025, Present · Dynamics and Control Lab, University of South Carolina
Robust Control Framework for Retractable Tether Multirotor UAVs
Developing a robust control framework for RTMUAVs, tackling the unsolved problem of maintaining stability through slack-to-taut tether transitions under real-world disturbances. This work has sharpened expertise in nonlinear dynamics, adaptive control, and high-fidelity simulation.

Overview

Tethered UAV (TMUAV) systems offer compelling advantages in persistent aerial operations, especially retractable tethers (RTMUAV). However they introduce a fundamentally difficult controls problem: the system must remain stable as the tether dynamically transitions between a slack (unconstrained) and taut (tension-loaded) state. Currently, a controller framework capable of handling both of these states and employing RTMUAV use in high risk situations does not exist. The goal of this project is to build a controller that is robust against these conditions, allowing for expanded use of RTMUAVs.

UAV Dynamics and System Modeling

  • Developed a full coupled dynamic model of the UAV, tether, and payload as a unified system, capturing the nonlinear interactions between all three bodies across both tether states
  • Tested and successfully controlled the UAV against disturbances triggering slack state mid-flight

Tether Modeling

  • Modeled the tether under both slack and taut conditions, incorporating state-switching logic with adaptive gains to handle the transition dynamics that destabilize conventional controllers
  • Used spring-damper model to accurately predict and model tether tension and dynamics
  • Multilink model in order to more accurately model tether behavior

Controller Design and Low Frequency Learning

The geometric controller uses adaptive gains to handle both tether states. The Low Frequency Learning (LFL) filter was developed as a key addition, filtering through high frequency noise by comparing it to a weight estimate, eliminating leakage bias. Compared to standard MRAC, LFL tracked much more precisely, required less control rate effort from the UAV, and achieved this at the same learning rate, proving to be an effective addition to the controller for more effective response.

RTMUAV system overview and general control demonstration
Multilink tether model and LFL controller performance study

Disturbance Modeling

  • 8-tone gust field applied throughout modeling and tests
  • Sensor noise included in tension feedback term
  • Ran randomized simulations and extracted 95th-percentile excursion envelopes across tether lengths of 2 to 6 m
  • Tested and compared results from 2D and 3D simulations

Goals

  • Properly address tether dynamics during reeling movements
  • Maintain stability in cases of slack to taut re-engagements and wind or other disturbances
  • Show tracking errors are within bounded errors for stability throughout mission horizon
  • Incorporate realistic uncertainties and disturbances to validate controller robustness

Certified Envelopes for RTMUAV Operation

  • Maneuver envelopes to replace fixed safety margins with maneuver-specific, validated clearance requirements
  • Tested with tether lengths from 2 to 6 meters, comparing 2D and 3D simulation results
  • Hover and Reel: longer tether and larger disturbance generally increase required clearance; full 3D validation shows the planar model needs a fidelity correction
  • Transit: envelope increases strongly with both speed and tether length; planning can trade transit speed against required clearance
  • Swing Arrest: peak excursion is similar in planar and 3D; gain sweep showed tuning helps but does not eliminate the discrepancy

Reeling Platform

  • Novel reeling system weighing under 500 to 800 g, tracking tether tension and direction in real time
  • Previously only done with ground-linked systems with heavy infrastructure, so scaling down was a significant challenge
  • Two-axis gimbal with rotary sensors measures tether direction
  • Reel-rotation sensor tracks tether deployment and retrieval
  • Provides the near-end tension value needed for onboard controller calculations
  • Key requirements: lightweight module and sensor package, required payload capability, commanded reel speed within available power budget, and onboard tension and direction measurements for flight testing

Oct 2026, Present · Personal Project
Scramjet Supersonic Isolator Unstart and Shock Train Transient Simulation
Transient compressible CFD of a supersonic isolator under controlled back-pressure forcing, built on steady-state conditions matched to a UT Austin isolator study.

Overview

Supersonic inlets and isolators are critical components in scramjet propulsion systems, tasked with diffusing high-speed airflow and establishing a stable pressure gradient prior to the combustor. However, back-pressure spikes can cause the internal shock train to migrate upstream, leading to complete inlet “unstart” and catastrophic thrust collapse. Steady-state behavior is well documented, but capturing the transient relaxation response and shock-train anchoring under specific forcing functions remains a difficult computational problem. The goal of this study is to model and analyze the transient unstart mechanics of a supersonic isolator under controlled forcing conditions to evaluate dynamic stability boundaries.

Compressible Flow and Solver Setup (ANSYS Fluent)

  • Full compressible Navier-Stokes formulation coupled with the energy equation to resolve severe thermal gradients and shock-boundary layer interactions (SBLI)
  • Ideal gas density law with temperature-dependent molecular viscosity to account for temperature and pressure changes through supersonic flow
  • k-omega SST turbulence model to capture boundary layer separation, large recirculation zones, and the steep adverse pressure gradients of internal shock trains
  • Fine time increments in the transient solver to resolve high-frequency pressure waves without numerical diffusion damping

Steady-State Validation Against UT Austin Study

  • Began by matching the steady-state operating conditions of the published UT Austin isolator study before running any transient cases
  • Used the converged steady-state solution as the initial condition for the transient simulations, so every transient result starts from a validated baseline

Linear Ramp Transient Simulation

  • Applied a linear ramp back-pressure forcing function to simulate progressive downstream throttling and induce gradual upstream shock-train migration
  • Captured the transient progression across 1,000 steps, showing the shock system moving from a nominal downstream position to an upstream-anchored separation structure
  • The results show a growing blockage that constricts the flow, affecting mass flow rate and shock development into the combustor

Step-Function Transient Simulation (In Progress)

  • Evaluates the system response to instantaneous back-pressure spikes rather than gradual ramps, simulating acute flow choking events
  • Essential for finding worst-case propagation speeds and critical time constants for active flow control design and vehicle survivability
  • Will benchmark relaxation time scales and hysteresis effects against the linear ramp baseline to establish operational safety boundaries

Jun 2026, Oct 2026 · Personal Project
CFD Study of Crosswind / Yaw Effect on Aerodynamics of Formula 1 Cars
Five OpenFOAM simulations across 0 to 8 degree yaw angles on a 2026-regulation F1 car model, studying how crosswinds affect downforce, drag, and aerodynamic center.

Overview

Formula 1 is a sport known for its engineering challenges and for maximizing performance out of designs, even for advantages of tenths of a second. Working in this kind of environment is something I would love to be a part of, so I pursued this personal summer project to more deeply explore the aerodynamics of Formula 1 cars, specifically studying the effect of crosswind yaw angles on downforce, drag, and aerodynamic balance.

Formula 1 Car Development

  • Based on 2026 F1 rules, with some lenient constraints
  • Focused on downforce and drag considerations for the underbody, front wing, and rear wing to ensure a proper model
  • Used CATIA Generative Shape Design to develop an extensive and precise CAD model for the car
  • Compared simple design with other teams to ensure proper modeling

CFD Setup

  • All results simulated through OpenFOAM
  • Formula 1 car moving at 80,000 mm/s; airflow velocity magnitude held constant across all yaw angles to conserve dynamic pressure
  • Ground velocity modeled only in y-velocity, matching the velocity of the car
  • Rotating MRFs around all four wheels
  • Inlet and exhaust not modeled, but kept enclosed due to lack of engine data
  • kOmegaSST turbulence model to properly catch vortex development, flow separation, and viscous effects that would change from yawing winds

General Results

  • CL and CD change proportionally, with CD increasing slightly faster
  • This effect causes a slight drift of the L/D ratio downward
  • Conclusion: crosswinds up to 8 degrees have no effect on overall L/D ratios of the car, with effects only on yawing moments, forces, and aerodynamic center
