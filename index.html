Jan 2025, Present · Dynamics and Control Lab, University of South Carolina
Robust Control Framework for Retractable Tether Multirotor UAVs
Developing a robust control framework for RTMUAVs, tackling the unsolved problem of maintaining stability through slack-to-taut tether transitions under real-world disturbances.

Overview

Retractable tether UAV systems offer compelling advantages for persistent aerial operations, but introduce a fundamentally difficult controls problem: the system must remain stable as the tether dynamically transitions between a slack (unconstrained) and taut (tension-loaded) state. No controller framework capable of handling both states robustly in high-risk situations existed prior to this work.

System Modeling

  • Built a fully coupled dynamic model of the UAV, tether, and payload as a unified system, capturing nonlinear interactions across both tether states
  • Tether modeled under slack and taut conditions using a spring-damper model and multilink representation
  • State-switching logic with adaptive gains to handle transition dynamics that destabilize conventional controllers

Controller Design

  • Geometric controller with adaptive gains validated in MATLAB against wind and measurement disturbances
  • Implemented Low Frequency Learning (LFL) filter, which outperforms standard MRAC in tracking precision while requiring less control rate effort
  • Python simulation environment mapping expected flight envelopes to safety and clearance regions

Disturbance Modeling

  • 8-tone gust field applied throughout testing
  • Sensor noise included in tension feedback term
  • Randomized simulations extracting 95th-percentile excursion envelopes across tether lengths of 2 to 6 m

Reeling Platform

  • Novel reeling system under 500 to 800 g, tracking tether tension and direction in real time
  • Two-axis gimbal with rotary sensors, and a reel-rotation sensor for deployment and retrieval tracking

Jun 2026, Oct 2026 · Personal Project
CFD Study of Crosswind / Yaw Effect on Aerodynamics of Formula 1 Cars
Five OpenFOAM simulations across 0 to 8 degree yaw angles on a 2026-regulation F1 car model, studying how crosswinds affect downforce, drag, and aerodynamic center.

Overview

Formula 1 is a sport known for its engineering challenges and for maximizing performance out of designs, even for advantages of tenths of a second. Working in this kind of environment is something I would love to be a part of, so I pursued this personal summer project to more deeply explore the aerodynamics of Formula 1 cars, specifically studying the effect of crosswind yaw angles on downforce, drag, and aerodynamic balance.

Formula 1 Car Development

  • Based on 2026 F1 rules, with some lenient constraints
  • Focused on downforce and drag considerations for the underbody, front wing, and rear wing to ensure a proper model
  • Used CATIA Generative Shape Design to develop an extensive and precise CAD model
  • Compared design with other teams to ensure proper modeling

CFD Setup

  • All results simulated through OpenFOAM
  • Car moving at 80,000 mm/s, with constant velocity magnitude held across yaw angles to conserve dynamic pressure
  • Ground velocity modeled only in y-velocity to match the velocity of the car
  • Rotating MRFs around all four wheels
  • Inlet and exhaust not modeled, but kept enclosed due to lack of engine data
  • kOmegaSST turbulence model to properly capture vortex development, flow separation, and viscous effects under yawing conditions

Results

  • CL and CD both change proportionally, with CD increasing slightly faster
  • This causes a slight downward drift in the L/D ratio
  • Conclusion: crosswinds up to 8 degrees have no effect on overall L/D ratios of the car, with effects only on yawing moments, forces, and aerodynamic center
